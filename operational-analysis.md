# Operational Analysis

## Executive Summary

The synthetic practice has enough overall appointment supply to support established-patient demand, but it does not allocate enough weekly capacity to new patients. Baseline new-patient demand is 78 requests per week compared with 59 dedicated new-patient slots, creating a structural deficit of 19 appointments per week.

With a starting backlog of 105 patients, the baseline model projects continued backlog growth over the next 12 weeks. The access problem therefore cannot be corrected by maintaining current templates and asking providers to work harder within the same allocation structure.

A second constraint exists in patient communication. Modeled call volume is 1,450 calls per week while three scheduling FTE provide capacity for approximately 1,260 calls. This means provider-side changes alone may still leave the patient-access system overloaded.

## Finding 1: The Primary Constraint Is Capacity Allocation

The practice has substantial established-patient capacity relative to modeled established demand, but too few appointment slots are reserved for new patients.

This means the problem is not simply a shortage of total provider time. It is partly a template-design problem.

### Leadership implication

Before adding expensive clinical FTE, leadership should test whether existing templates can be rebalanced toward new-patient access while maintaining adequate established-patient availability.

## Finding 2: Template Rebalancing Helps but Does Not Fully Solve the Problem

The template-rebalance scenario shifts 14 weekly slots toward new patients without adding total provider capacity.

That materially reduces the weekly new-patient deficit, but new-patient demand still exceeds supply. The backlog therefore continues to grow, only at a slower rate.

### Leadership implication

Rebalancing should be part of the solution, but not the only intervention.

## Finding 3: Adding Clinical Capacity Alone Leaves an Administrative Bottleneck

Evening access sessions and added APP capacity both improve appointment supply. However, baseline scheduling capacity remains below weekly call demand.

Without additional scheduling support or workflow redesign, the organization could create more appointment inventory while still making it difficult for patients to access that inventory.

### Leadership implication

Capacity decisions should include front-end access operations, not only provider schedules.

## Finding 4: The Recommended Combined Scenario Addresses Both Constraints

The recommended model combines:

- template rebalancing;
- targeted evening access capacity;
- 0.5 additional scheduling FTE.

This increases weekly new-patient capacity enough to exceed modeled demand, protects established-patient capacity above modeled established demand, and closes most of the scheduling call-capacity gap.

Over the 12-week model period, the new-patient backlog declines instead of growing.

## Leadership Recommendations

### 1. Rebalance appointment templates before adding full clinical FTE
Identify providers and sessions where established-patient capacity can be shifted to new-patient access without pushing established demand beyond safe thresholds.

### 2. Add targeted access sessions instead of broad schedule expansion
Use evening or designated access sessions where demand is concentrated. This creates incremental supply without immediately committing to a full new provider.

### 3. Align scheduling staffing with access expansion
If appointment capacity grows, make sure the scheduling team can absorb the associated call and booking volume.

### 4. Review access as a system
Track new-patient demand, appointment supply, backlog, call volume, scheduling FTE, template utilization, no-shows, and established-patient capacity together.

### 5. Establish intervention thresholds
Examples could include:

- new-patient demand exceeding capacity for two consecutive weeks;
- backlog exceeding a defined number of days of capacity;
- call demand exceeding modeled staffing capacity;
- established-patient capacity falling below projected demand after template changes.

## Portfolio Note

This analysis uses entirely synthetic data. It demonstrates capacity planning, access strategy, operational modeling, and executive decision support without using patient information, employer data, or proprietary materials.
